Staff Judgement: When (Not) to Event-Source & the ADR

◷ ~20 min read • Level 5 · Staff judgement • Prereq: Lesson 15 · Migrating from CRUD

Everything so far makes you a strong implementer. This lesson is what makes you Staff: deciding (often against event sourcing), making trade-offs explicit in business language, writing the ADR reviewers trust, catching anti-patterns in other people’s designs, and making a whole organisation effective with the pattern. It closes with the Ledgerly capstone, a self-assessment and an 8-week plan.

Lighter alternatives that capture ~70% of the value

The Staff move is often “no”

Proposing the lighter alternative with a convincing argument is a stronger signal than building an event-sourced system. “We need an audit trail” is answered by an audit table in the same transaction — unless the audit must be the truth that can’t drift.

Say it in business language

“We trade 1–2 seconds of search staleness for a complete, regulator-grade decision history and the ability to answer new analytics questions retroactively.” No mention of aggregates, upcasters or projections — just what the business gives and gets.

4. Writing the ADR

A Staff engineer turns a decision into a durable, reviewable artefact. Note especially the exit plan and success metrics — they’re what make reviewers trust a bold architecture decision.

# ADR-042: Use event sourcing for the Claims bounded context

## Status
Proposed — 2026-10-22

## Context
- Regulators (e.g. local insurance authority) require full reconstruction of every claim decision.
- Current CRUD + audit table drifted from reality in 3 incidents in 2025 (links).
- Product wants 4 new analytics views on claim handling behaviour; data is not captured today.
- Team: 6 engineers, 1 with prior ES experience. Postgres is our standard datastore.

## Decision
- Event-source the Claim aggregate only. Policy, Customer and Document contexts stay CRUD.
- Event store: Postgres `events` table (append-only, optimistic concurrency via unique (stream, version)),
  global ordering via tx-id visibility filter.
- Read models: async projections in Postgres; one inline projection (ClaimStatus) for read-your-writes.
- Integration: public `claims.v1` Avro events on Kafka via store subscription; internal events never exposed.
- PII: no PII in events; reference CustomerId. Free-text notes encrypted with per-claim key (crypto-shredding).

## Consequences
+ Complete audit; temporal queries; new projections backfillable.
− Learning curve: 2-day workshop + pairing; ES champions in team.
− Eventual consistency in claim search (SLO: projection lag p99 < 2s).
− Must maintain upcasters; CI corpus of historic events.

## Alternatives considered
1. CRUD + transactional outbox + audit table — rejected: audit drift risk, no behavioural history.
2. Axon Framework/Server — deferred: adds framework + infra lock-in; revisit if throughput > 2k cmds/s.
3. KurrentDB — deferred: new infra to operate; revisit if subscription management becomes a burden.

## Reversibility / exit plan
Projections can materialise full current state into CRUD tables; we can stop appending and switch writes
to those tables if ES is abandoned (estimated 3–4 weeks).

## Success metrics (review in 6 months)
- Projection lag p99 < 2 s; rehydration p99 < 20 ms; zero parked events.
- Time to add a new read model < 1 sprint.
- Audit requests answered from projections, no engineer involvement.

ContextBusiness drivers with evidence (incidents, regulation), team reality

5. Design-review question bank

Use these when reviewing someone else’s ES design — or to pre-empt them in your own.

AreaQuestions
Domain & boundaries1. Why does this context need ES? What business question does history answer?
2. Aggregates and invariants? Expected events-per-stream over the lifetime?
3. Which invariants span aggregates; how are they enforced (saga, reservation, DCB, accepted risk)?
Events4. Show me five event names. Do they read like the business narrative?
5. Versioning strategy? Where do upcasters live? How is compatibility tested in CI?
6. Where is PII; how is erasure honoured across store, projections, snapshots, Kafka, backups?
Consistency & ordering7. How is optimistic concurrency enforced? Retry policy for conflicts?
8. How is the global order gap-free / commit-ordered for subscribers?
9. Which reads need read-your-writes, and how is it provided?
Operations10. How long does the largest projection take to rebuild? Procedure? Who’s on call?
11. SLOs for projection lag? Which alerts exist?
12. Poison event: where is it parked, who’s paged?
13. Can we restore the store and rebuild all projections consistently? Drilled?
Integration & evolution14. Which events are public? Contract format and compatibility mode?
15. What’s the exit strategy if ES turns out to be wrong?

6. Organisational & people aspects (the actual Staff job)

LeverWhat you actually do
Scope it deliberatelyWrite the ADR; get explicit buy-in from product (eventual consistency) and compliance (GDPR approach).
Create a paved roadA small internal library: event-store client, decider base types, projection runner, upcaster registry, test DSL — so teams don’t each reinvent the gap bug.
Grow championsRun Event Storming workshops; pair on the first aggregates; write a “how we do ES here” guide with examples from your domain.
Operational ownershipProjections, rebuilds and subscriptions get runbooks and on-call ownership like any database.
Measure success honestlyIf after 6 months nobody uses history, temporal queries or new projections — be the one who says “we should simplify”.
Business languageCommunicate trade-offs as staleness vs history vs cost, never as framework features.

7. Real-world references worth studying

  • Accounting / banking cores — double-entry ledgers are the archetype; many modern core-banking and payments platforms are event-sourced or ledger-based.
  • LMAX architecture — an in-memory business logic processor that recovers state by replaying an input event log: ES for extreme low latency (Fowler’s write-up).
  • Git — content-addressed history plus a derived working tree; branches are alternative histories.
  • Kafka Streams / ksqlDB changelogs, database WALs — event sourcing at the infrastructure level.
  • Axon Framework and KurrentDB (EventStoreDB) case studies — vendor material; read critically. They show possibility, not proof that ES is right for you.

8. Capstone: build “Ledgerly”

An event-sourced wallet service. Aim for production shape, not features. Start from the Postgres event store in the lab README and grow it into Ledgerly.

# run locally (Podman, not Docker)
cd ../assets/lab && podman compose up -d   # the lab's Postgres event store (port 55432)
# for Ledgerly, extend your own compose.yml with Kafka (+ Schema Registry for Avro)
export DOCKER_HOST="unix://$(podman machine inspect --format '{{.ConnectionInfo.PodmanSocket.Path}}')"
./mvnw verify                              # Testcontainers against the Podman socket
Ledgerly requirements — tick each off
  • Wallet decider with Given/When/Then tests and a jqwik property test (balance never negative).
  • Postgres event store with optimistic concurrency; concurrent-withdraw test passes.
  • Transfer between two wallets via an event-sourced process manager with compensation and timeout.
  • Three projections; checkpoint in same transaction; idempotency test (replay duplicates).
  • Gap-safe global reads (tx-id visibility filter) + a test with interleaved transactions.
  • Blue/green rebuild of DailyVolume v1 → v2 with alias swap.
  • Read-your-writes via position token on GET /wallets/{id}/statement.
  • Upcaster: change MoneyDeposited.amount from number to {value, currency}; historical-event corpus test.
  • Crypto-shredding of a memo field; erasure endpoint.
  • Publisher to Kafka with idempotent producer, keyed by wallet id.
  • Snapshots — only after you measure rehydration time with 100k events and decide it’s needed.
  • Metrics: projection lag, append latency, conflict rate, events per stream (Micrometer → Prometheus).
  • An ADR for the project and a 1-page runbook (“projection stuck”, “rebuild”, “poison event”).
  • Run everything locally with Podman (podman compose up) and Testcontainers against the Podman socket.

Stretch: implement the same Transfer invariant with DCB (a single append with a tag-based condition) and write a one-page comparison note.

9. Self-assessment: are you Staff on this topic?

You’re there when you can, without notes:

  • Explain ES, CQRS and EDA and how they differ, in two minutes, to a product manager.
  • Write a decider for a new domain with pure decide/evolve and full GWT tests.
  • Design an event store schema that provides optimistic concurrency and gap-free global ordering.
  • Explain why Kafka is (usually) not your event store, and when it can be.
  • Design projections that are idempotent, checkpointed and rebuildable; run a zero-downtime rebuild.
  • Offer at least four UX/API strategies for eventual consistency.
  • Choose between weak schema, upcasting, new event type and copy-transform for a given change.
  • Design GDPR erasure for an event-sourced system end-to-end.
  • Spot ten anti-patterns in a design doc.
  • Decide not to use ES, and propose the lighter alternative, with a convincing argument.
  • Write an ADR with alternatives, consequences, exit plan and success metrics.
  • Facilitate an Event Storming session that produces aggregate boundaries.

Final advice

The Staff signal is not “I built an event-sourced system”. It’s “I understood why our domain needed (or didn’t need) it, made the trade-offs explicit, made it safe to operate, and made other engineers effective with it.”
