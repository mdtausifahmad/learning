Technical Skills · Reference 04

Strategy & Vision Cheat Sheet

Compressed from Lesson 04•Print-friendly
Mindset

Strategy is useful only if it changes what teams do when you are not in the room. If a policy implies no trade-off, it is an aspiration, not a strategy.

Rumelt’s kernel

PartDefinitionEngineering example
DiagnosisThe challenge and why it is hardCross-region launches are unsafe because data ownership is ambiguous
Guiding policyThe chosen approach — a trade-offOne owning service per customer domain; derived views via events
Coherent actionCoordinated steps that implement itOwnership map, migrate three tables, event contracts, block new shared writes

Which document is it?

ArtifactHorizonAnswers
Design docOne projectHow will we build this, and what did we reject?
RoadmapQuartersWhat ships when? (no diagnosis, no trade-off)
Strategy~6–18 monthsWhat is today’s key challenge and which trade-off do we choose?
Vision~2–3 yearsWhat plausible, optimistic future are we steering toward?

A vision without strategy is a poster; a strategy without vision can optimise the wrong future.

The Staff-engineer recipe

Weak → strong

Weak (everyone agrees, nobody acts)Strong (constrains decisions)
Improve platform reliability.Tier-one APIs adopt 99.9% SLOs, dependency timeouts and 10-minute rollback before new feature work.
Teams should own their services.One service owner and one data owner per customer domain; no new shared DB writes after Q2.
Modernise the frontend.New checkout uses the design system; legacy checkout gets compliance fixes only.

Good strategy is specific, opinionated, boring, evidence-backed and enforceable. It shows its work: evidence, rejected alternatives, risks, why now.

Guiding-policy types (right altitude: not slogans, not task lists)

TypeExample
Resource allocation30% of platform capacity on migration until shared writes are gone
Priority ruleData correctness beats launch speed for billing flows
Fundamental ruleNo new synchronous calls from checkout to experimental services
Escalation ruleExceptions need domain-lead and SRE approval
SequencingWhat must land before other work becomes safe or useful

A good policy makes some teams unhappy in understandable ways.

When to write one

  • The same debate recurs 3–4 times — you need a policy, not another meeting.
  • Teams solve one problem inconsistently, or a migration needs coordinated adoption.
  • Incidents reveal systemic risk, or product direction invalidated old assumptions.
  • Leaders must choose between mutually exclusive investments, or the future is too hazy to invest.
  • Not every project — many need only a design doc, an ADR or a roadmap item.

Vision doc (1–2 pages)

  • Users whose lives are better, and the business capabilities enabled.
  • Engineering experience and technical shape of the future state.
  • Non-goals — what is intentionally not part of it.
  • Believable milestones; optimistic, not audacious.
  • Concrete — “launch a compliant payment method in one week without platform support” beats a slogan like “a great payments platform”.

Enforcement toolkit

Make the strategy the path of least resistance: architecture review criteria, funding gates, paved roads and platform defaults, SLO policy, migration dashboards, RFC templates, dependency rules, executive sponsorship. Know when to persuade, when to set defaults, and when to escalate.

Reading someone else’s strategy

  • Diagnosis? What trade-off does the policy impose?
  • Coherent actions? What is deprioritised?
  • Enforcement? What evidence would prove it wrong?
Traps
  • Generic principles everyone agrees with and no one executes.
  • A roadmap labelled “strategy” with no diagnosis or trade-off.
  • Publishing without changing funding, review or escalation.
  • Skipping bottom-up evidence, then being surprised by resistance.
  • Treating exceptions as failure instead of calibration data.
