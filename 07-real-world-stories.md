# 07 · Real-World Stories & Lessons

Ten first-person accounts of engineers who reached Staff/Principal. Read these when you need proof it's doable — and a reality check on *how* it actually happens. Every one converges on the same levers: **visibility, sponsorship, a big ambiguous project, and shifting from code to influence.**

---

## The stories

### Bert Fan — Senior Staff, Slack[^1]
His explicit **5-step path**: (1) build mutual trust with your manager by delivering → (2) they advocate for you to lead high-impact projects → (3) deliver successfully → (4) the project has significant company impact → (5) promotion becomes easy to advocate for. Blunt caveat: *"Reaching Staff is a combination of luck, timing, and work"* and *"This is not a meritocracy and your professional network is important."* Advice: shift from "writing better code" to "working better with other people… **try to become the engineer that people want to work with.**"

### Ritu Vincent — Staff, Dropbox[^2]
Became Staff as a **tech lead** on an 18-month project (re-architecting the desktop client for multi-user — "from the kernel all the way to the UI"). Top factors: **visibility** (ran the intern program, moderated dozens of hiring debriefs) + **sponsorship** (great relationship with manager *and* skip-level). Advice: **ask for the hard, ambiguous projects, not the ones you'll ace** — and **delegate the *hardest* pieces** to grow the team, don't hoard them. Be radically honest with your manager.

### Katie Sylor-Miller — Frontend Architect (Staff II), Etsy[^3]
**Nominated twice and rejected before getting it on the third try.** Noticed design-system components copy-pasted everywhere and proactively built a reusable framework. What finally worked: *"I had a good sponsor in my Director… those are the people in the room making the decision. It's not your peers and it's not really your manager."* Most-quoted line: *"You've probably already got the technical chops; what you need to do is work on your reputation… people expect it to be a meritocracy, when it's really not."*

### Dan Na — Staff & Team Lead, Squarespace[^4]
Shipped an alternative frontend architecture **and owned the communication/education** (talks, status emails, blog post). Also built cultural glue (a coffee-pairing Slack bot, a management book club) — *"part of being an effective Staff-plus engineer is caring about cultural gaps as much as technical gaps."* Framing: *"Progressing up the ladder is an additive exercise in forcing you to care about more things… **You're a leader now, whether you want to be or not.**"*

### Joy Ebertz — Senior Staff, Split (ex-Box)[^5]
Got Staff **without a single "Staff project"** — via a fast-growing company, **visibility** (blogging, speaking), and joining an **API standards committee** that cross-cut many teams. Best advice: **amplify your strengths** — as "a giant introvert" she used writing/blogging to build her network instead of meetups. Write your promo case *early* to expose gaps; the committee "looks for what isn't said."

### Keavy McMinn — Senior Principal, Fastly (ex-GitHub)[^6]
The clearest **"switch companies"** story: *"the biggest factor was changing companies. The type of work didn't dramatically change, but changing companies enabled me to get the title."* Public speaking was "a huge factor." On the "prove it again" cliché — she calls it bad advice. For new Staff+: **find your peers / support network** — "it gets lonely the higher up you go."

### Diana Pojar — Staff Data Engineer, Slack[^7]
Joined a **newly-formed team** (build-from-scratch = org-wide impact). Pivotal work moved ~25% of load off production MySQL ("saving the company millions") and owned how business metrics were computed for IPO readiness. Top factor: **belief** — *"the main factor is to actually believe that YOU CAN DO IT and don't let impostor syndrome win."* Reframes stress as a growth signal. *"What got you to senior will not get you to Staff+."*

### Michelle Bu — Payments Products Tech Lead, Stripe[^8]
A humbling non-linear start: rated **L2 after 2.5 years** ("pretty disappointed"), had a **1.5-year project canceled** — then **Senior → Staff → CPO-report in 3 years.** The insight: in those "slow" early years she built *relationships* (helping users, spinning up new engineers) that later *accelerated* her. *"The softer skills, connections across the company, user focus… are what ultimately helped me accelerate my path to Staff after I built my technical foundation."* Heavy on **writing** (evergreen "top 3" doc, public decision logs).

### Nelson Elhage — formerly Staff, Stripe[^9]
His #1 factor: **broad, early context** as a compounding advantage — *"I tried to have an unusually global perspective. That helped me know which problems were important."* Became "a router of information and ideas." Wary of the "Staff project" myth (many great Staff engineers are "gurus and routers"). Advice: **focus** — *"pick one of [thirty things] at a time and really focus rather than pushing a little across many."* Invest in relationships with eng leadership.

### Shruti Kapoor — Staff (ex-PayPal, ex-Slack)[^10]
Thesis: *"It's not just about what you do — it's about **who knows** what you do."* Core distinction: *"Senior Engineers are **given** projects, Staff Engineers **make** projects."* Her 5 reasons you're not promoted yet (+ fixes): (1) waiting to be told you're ready → perform at the level now; (2) impostor syndrome → keep a brag doc; (3) waiting to become a deep expert → "learn a little about many things"; (4) avoiding "politics" → reframe self-advocacy as **data sharing**; (5) weak networking → one coffee chat/month. Ships a concrete **6-month plan** (see [03](03-career-progression-plan.md)).

## Cross-cutting patterns (what repeatedly made the difference)

1. **Visibility/reputation over raw skill** — "it's not just what you do, it's who knows what you do." Nearly universal. Most had the technical chops already.
2. **A trusting sponsor relationship** — with the manager *and* skip-level/director. The director "in the room" is who actually decides.
3. **A large, ambiguous, cross-team project** with measurable company impact — *or* an equivalent track record (committee/standards work, guru/router role).
4. **The job stops being about code** — it becomes influence without authority, communication, and picking the right problems.
5. **Writing as a force multiplier** — docs, decision logs, blogs, talks (especially for introverts and remote workers).
6. **Belief + tolerance for ambiguity** — impostor syndrome and "everything is now vague" are the norm, not a signal you don't belong.

## Honest lessons & surprises

- **The role gives you *less* control, not more.** *"There's a misconception that you become a Staff Engineer and then you'll be in control… That's absolutely the opposite of what happens! All of a sudden, everything is vague and ambiguous… you're responsible for finding the right problems, then convincing people they're important."*[^3]
- **You become lonelier.** *"I'm definitely feeling lonelier at work now."* People start looking to you for an opinion — your silence gets over-interpreted.[^8]
- **The ladder can be humbling and fair in hindsight.** Michelle Bu: *"L2 was 100% fair"* — and the "slow" relationship-building period is what later accelerated her.[^8]
- **Delegate the *hard* parts, not just the easy ones.**[^2]
- **Some standard advice is bad advice** — e.g., the "now prove it again" hero-narrative cliché.[^6]
- **Stress = a growth signal.** *"When I'm feeling stressed or unsure of my abilities, that's often a sign that I'm growing."*[^7]
- **A management detour isn't wasted**, but may slow the specific Staff milestone.[^5]
- **Titles can be partly circumstantial** — acquisition inflation, or changing companies, can grant a title the same work wouldn't.[^6][^9]

> ⚠️ **Survivorship bias caveat:** these are self-selected successes. Several explicitly flag **luck, timing, sponsorship, and privilege** as real factors. Treat them as *patterns to emulate*, not guarantees.
