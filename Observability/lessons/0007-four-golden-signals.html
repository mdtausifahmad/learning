Reliability & SRE · Lesson 07

The Four Golden Signals

◷ ~11 min read • Level: Foundations • Prereq: Lesson 06 — the three signals

Lesson 06 said metrics, logs, and traces exist. But what do you actually measure? Instrument everything and you drown in noise and cardinality bills; instrument nothing and you’re blind. The four golden signals are the canonical answer to “if you could measure only a few things about a user-facing system, measure these.”

1. First frame: symptoms vs. causes

Good monitoring answers two questions: what’s broken (the symptom) and why (the cause). The SRE book calls this “one of the most important distinctions in writing good monitoring.” You page on symptoms (user impact) and debug with causes. The golden signals are mostly symptom metrics — measured from the user’s side, which is exactly where your SLIs live.

2. The four signals

SignalWhat it measures
LatencyTime to serve a request. Measure success and error latency separately (below).
TrafficDemand on the system — req/s for web, sessions for streaming, txns/s for a store.
ErrorsRate of failures: explicit (500s), implicit (200 + wrong content), or by policy (over 1 s = error).
SaturationHow “full” the most-constrained resource is. Set a utilization target — systems degrade before 100%.
Don’t average error latency away

A fast 500 (DB connection refused, returned in 3 ms) mixed into your latency makes the numbers look great — misleading. But a slow error is worse than a fast one, so don’t just filter errors out either. Track the latency of successful and failed requests separately.

Saturation is the subtle one

Because systems degrade before 100%, saturation is a leading indicator. Rising p99 latency is often the earliest saturation signal; it’s also predictive (“this disk fills in 4 hours”). Watch it — but page on it only when it’s nearly problematic, not on every wiggle.

3. Two lenses that specialise the signals: RED & USE

LensScopeSignalsUse for
Golden signalsa user-facing serviceLatency · Traffic · Errors · Saturationthe baseline dashboard
RED (Tom Wilkie)per request-driven serviceRate · Errors · Durationevery service (user view)
USE (Brendan Gregg)per resourceUtilisation · Saturation · ErrorsCPU, memory, disk, network (causes)

They fit together: RED is the user-symptom view (≈ golden signals minus saturation), USE is the resource-cause view. RED tells you the checkout is slow; USE tells you the database’s disk queue is saturated. “For every resource, check utilisation, saturation, and errors.”

Low utilisation ≠ no saturation

A resource can average 70% utilisation and still saturate: a burst hits 100% for seconds, queues form, latency spikes — and the five-minute average hides it entirely. Averages lie here too. Use short-window p99s and queue depth, not just mean utilisation.

4. Design the telemetry (the staff-level part)

  • Name with conventions. Use OpenTelemetry semantic conventions (http.server.request.duration, http.response.status_code). Stable, standard names mean your dashboards and alerts survive refactors.
  • Bound your cardinality. Good metric labels are low-cardinality: method, route (templated — /users/{id}, not /users/12345), status_class. Never user_id, raw paths, or timestamps — push that detail into traces/logs.
  • Histogram the tail. Never mean latency — bucket by latency (exponential boundaries, ~×3) so you can read p99. Remember: the p99 of one backend can become the median of your frontend.
  • Right resolution. Per-minute CPU misses second-long spikes that drive tail latency; sample/bucket internally to catch them cheaply.

5. It all connects back

Your SLIs (Lesson 02) are golden signals: availability = Errors, latency SLI = Latency. You alert on those user-facing symptoms via burn rate (Lesson 05); saturation and USE metrics are your diagnostic and predictive signals — kept for debugging and capacity, not for paging on every wiggle. Symptom pages, cause metrics debug.

Try it in your own system
  • Pick one service. Write down its four golden signals and where each comes from.
  • List its key resources (CPU, memory, DB connections) and their USE metrics.
  • Audit your metric labels: any unbounded ones (user_id, raw paths)? Templatise them.
  • Confirm latency is a histogram, and that errors aren’t polluting success latency.
