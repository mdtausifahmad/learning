Reliability & SRE · Lesson 04

The Error-Budget Policy

◷ ~10 min read • Level: Foundations → Judgment • Prereq: Lesson 03 — you have a budget

A budget nobody acts on is just a number on a dashboard — that’s the “SLO without teeth” rung you’re trying to climb past. The error-budget policy is the teeth: a written, pre-agreed rule for what the organisation does as the budget drains. This is the staff-level heart of SRE — where a metric becomes organisational behaviour.

1. The gap a policy closes

You computed a budget in Lesson 03. But what actually happens when it runs low? Without a pre-agreed answer, every near-miss is re-litigated in the moment — and the loudest voice, or the shipping deadline, usually wins. The policy’s whole job is to decide in advance, calmly, before anyone is under incident pressure.

Not a punishment

Google’s own policy says it plainly: halting change is undesirable, and the policy is not a punishment for missing an SLO. It gives teams permission to focus exclusively on reliability when the data shows reliability now matters more than the next feature. It’s a blameless control mechanism, not a stick.

2. The core rule: ship until you can’t

The spine of every error-budget policy is two states:

  • At or above SLO — budget healthy → releases proceed as normal. Ship, experiment, spend the budget. That’s what it’s for.
  • Budget exhausted (over the rolling window) → halt all changes and releases except P0 incidents and security fixes, until the service is back within SLO.

The green and red ends are the Google model. The amber middle — slow down before you hit zero — is a common extension that burn-rate alerting makes possible (Lesson 05).

That freeze is the mechanism that makes product and engineering share the cost of reliability. Suddenly “ship the feature vs. fix stability” isn’t a debate — the budget already decided.

3. It depends on the cause

A good policy doesn’t freeze blindly on any budget miss — it asks whose reliability problem is this? Straight from Google’s example:

The team must stop and fix reliability if…The team may keep shipping features if…
A code bug or procedural error in your service burned the budget. A company-wide network problem caused the outage.
A postmortem reveals a chance to soften a hard dependency. Another team’s service failed — and they’ve frozen to fix it.
Mis-categorised errors hid spend that would have missed the SLO. Budget was burned by out-of-scope users (load tests, pentesters).

The principle: the freeze exists to fix reliability you can control — not to punish you for a provider’s bad day.

4. Triggers, escalation, and teeth

  • Postmortem trigger. If a single incident burns more than 20% of the four-week budget, a postmortem is mandatory — with at least one P0 action item on the root cause. (A recurring class of outage >20% per quarter earns a P0 on quarterly planning.)
  • Escalation path. Disagreements about the budget calculation or the required actions escalate to a named executive (in Google’s example, the CTO).
  • Executive sign-off is the teeth. A policy an exec has approved can’t be waved away by a shipping deadline. Without that backing, you’re back to the loudest voice.
Why it’s worth the friction

Changes cause roughly 70% of outages. The error budget is the control that diverts attention to stability exactly when the data demands it — and no sooner. That’s how you ship fast and stay reliable, instead of trading one off against the other by gut feel.

5. What makes a policy real

  • Written & specific — concrete actions, not “we’ll be careful”.
  • Approved by a senior owner with authority to enforce the freeze.
  • Has an escalation path for disagreements — up, not sideways.
  • Names its exceptions (P0/security; out-of-scope traffic) — sparingly, or it loses teeth.
  • Has a revisit date — it’s a living agreement, not a stone tablet.

Try it in your own system

Draft a one-page error-budget policy for your service. Include:

  • The goal (protect users; balance reliability vs features; not punishment).
  • Action at/above SLO and action when exhausted (P0/security exception).
  • The cause-based must-fix vs. may-continue split.
  • The >20% postmortem trigger, the escalation owner, and a revisit date.
