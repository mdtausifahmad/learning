Setting the SLO & the Error Budget

◷ ~11 min read • Level: Foundations • Prereq: Lesson 02 — you have an SLI

You can choose a good SLI. Now you put a number and a window on it — and that turns reliability into a bank account you can spend. This lesson is where the abstract “error budget” becomes a concrete count of failures you’re allowed, and a document your team signs.

1. Pick the target: from data first, judgment second

Don’t invent the number in a meeting. Measure your SLI over a few weeks, then round to a clean, achievable starting point. The Workbook’s rule of thumb: round availability down to two significant figures, and round latency thresholds up to the nearest ~50 ms.

A real worked example (from the SRE Workbook), measured over four weeks on an API:

Measured→ Starter SLO
3,663,253 requests; 97.123% succeeded97% availability
90th-percentile latency = 432 ms90% of requests < 450 ms
99th-percentile latency = 891 ms99% of requests < 900 ms

Notice the two latency thresholds — one pins the typical experience, one pins the tail (Lesson 02). And notice you use histogram_quantile here to read the p90/p99 and pick the threshold — then you express the SLO itself as a proportion-under-threshold ratio (Reference 03).

The current-performance trap

Starting from measured performance is fine — if you iterate. But don’t permanently enshrine current numbers: users come to expect whatever you deliver, so an over-tight SLO commits you to defending speed you never promised. Set the bar at “above this, almost all users are happy”, then refine.

2. Pick the window: rolling, in whole weeks

An SLO needs a window. Two families, and they’re for different jobs:

Rolling windowCalendar window
e.g.trailing 4 weeksthis month / quarter
Best forthe SLO that drives engineering decisionsbusiness reporting & SLAs
Whytracks recent user experience; no artificial resetaligns with planning cycles
Watch out—“budget resets Jan 1” gaming; mid-quarter you can’t know future traffic
Google’s default

A four-week rolling window is the good general-purpose choice. Use whole weeks so every window has the same number of weekends — a 30-day window swings between four and five weekends, making your SLI wobble for boring reasons. Complement it with weekly summaries (course-correction) and quarterly reports (project planning).

3. Compute the error budget

Now the budget stops being a percentage and becomes a count you can spend:

error budget (events)  =  (1 − SLO) × valid events in the window

Applying it to the worked example’s four weeks (Table 2-4 in the Workbook):

SLOAllowed failures / 4 weeks
97% availability109,897 failed requests
90% faster than 450 ms366,325 slow requests
99% faster than 900 ms36,632 slow requests

This is the number that makes reliability operable. Say a 99.9% SLO on 3,000,000 requests gives a budget of 3,000 failures. A single outage that burns 1,500 errors just spent half your budget for the whole window:

budget remaining · 1,500
spent by one outage · 1,500
0error budget = 3,000 failures / 4 weeks3,000

Suddenly “was that outage a big deal?” has a crisp answer: it cost 50% of our month. As a time intuition, 99.9% over four weeks (40,320 min) is a budget of about 40 minutes of full failure. (Reference 01’s table used a 30-day window — 43 min — so always state the window with the budget.)

4. Reading the budget: spent, remaining, burn

Three quantities you’ll watch:

  • Spent = failures so far this window.
  • Remaining = budget − spent. When it hits zero, you’re out.
  • Burn rate = how fast you’re spending, relative to “even” pace. Burn exactly 1× would empty the budget precisely at window’s end; 10× empties it in a tenth of the window.

Burn rate is what you actually alert on — a page should fire when you’re burning fast enough to threaten the budget, not on every blip. We build that alerting in Lesson 04.

5. Make it real: the SLO document & sign-off

An SLO isn’t real until it’s written down and agreed. A minimal SLO document records: the SLI (specification and implementation), the target, the window, the rationale, and the error-budget policy (what happens when it’s exhausted — Lesson 04). Then get three parties to sign:

  • Product agrees the target is “good enough” for users — below it is worth spending engineering time to fix.
  • Developers agree that when the budget is spent, they’ll shift to reducing risk until it recovers.
  • Ops/SRE agree the SLO is defensible without heroics, toil, or burnout.
Sign-off is the real test

If the SRE can’t defend the target without burning out, it’s too strict. If product can’t stomach the velocity hit of defending it, it’s too strict too. The negotiation is the value — it forces an explicit, shared decision about how reliable to be.

Try it in your own system
  • Measure (or estimate) your SLI over ~4 weeks; round down to a starter availability SLO.
  • Pick two latency thresholds from your p90 / p99 (round up to ~50 ms).
  • Choose a window — default to 4-week rolling.
  • Compute the error budget in requests: (1 − SLO) × valid events.
  • Ask: what % of that budget would a single 30-minute full outage spend?

Bring these numbers — in Lesson 04 we turn the budget into a ship/freeze policy and a burn-rate alert.
