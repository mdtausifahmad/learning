Reliability & SRE · Lesson 02

Choosing Good SLIs

◷ ~11 min read • Level: Foundations • Prereq: Lesson 01 — SLI/SLO/error budget

Last lesson: reliability is a number. But a number of what? Pick the wrong signal and every SLO, alert, and error budget you build measures the wrong thing — green dashboards while users rage. This lesson is the concrete skill of choosing SLIs that actually track user happiness. It’s the raw material for everything downstream.

1. Quick recall, then the trap

From Lesson 01, in your own words: an SLO is a target for an SLI over a window, and the error budget is 1 − SLO. Hold that. Now the single most common SLI mistake:

The measure-what’s-easy trap

New teams reach for what’s easy to graph — CPU, memory, host uptime, pod counts, queue depth. All of these can look perfectly healthy while users can’t check out. They measure the machine, not the experience. An SLI must be a proxy for user happiness, measured from the user’s side.

2. The shape: good events ÷ valid events

Google’s strong recommendation: express every SLI as a ratio of two counts — good events divided by valid events, as a percentage. 

            good events
SLI  =  ───────────────────  × 100%
            valid events
  • Valid events = the events you’re accountable for (e.g. real user requests; you might exclude malformed junk or health-checks).
  • Good events = the valid events that met the bar (succeeded, or were fast enough).

Why this exact shape is worth insisting on — three properties fall out for free:

  • It ranges 0%–100%: 0 means nothing works, 100 means nothing is broken. Intuitive.
  • It composes with the error budget directly: budget = 100% − SLO, in the same units.
  • It’s uniform: every SLI becomes numerator / denominator / threshold, so one set of alerting, budget, and reporting tooling works for all of them.

In your stack, an availability SLI is just a ratio of counters in Prometheus:

# Availability SLI — successful ÷ all valid checkout requests, 28-day window
sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout", code!~"5.."}[28d]))
  /
sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[28d]))

And a latency SLI reads a histogram — the proportion faster than a threshold:

# Latency SLI — proportion of requests faster than 300 ms
sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="checkout", le="0.3"}[28d]))
  /
sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="checkout"}[28d]))

3. The menu: pick the SLI type by component

You don’t invent SLIs from scratch. Classify each part of your system into one of three component types, then pick from a known menu (from the SRE Workbook):

ComponentSLI types on the menu
Request-driven
API, web app		 Availability (proportion of requests that succeed) · Latency (proportion faster than a threshold) · Quality (proportion served un-degraded)
Pipeline
batch, stream, ETL		 Freshness (proportion of data newer than a threshold) · Correctness · Coverage
Storage Durability (data survives) · Latency · Throughput
Your starting pair

Your services are mostly request-driven. So begin with the two workhorses: availability and latency. Those two SLIs cover most of what your users actually feel. Add quality or freshness later, only where a component demands it.

4. Specification vs. implementation (the sharp idea)

Separate what you want to measure from how you measure it. This one distinction prevents most SLI arguments:

  • SLI specification — the user outcome that matters, independent of measurement. “Ratio of home-page loads under 100 ms.”
  • SLI implementation — the specification plus a specific way to measure it. One spec can have several implementations, each with different accuracy and cost.

Judge an implementation on three axes: quality (how faithfully it captures the real experience), coverage (how many users it represents), and cost. The load balancer sees more truth than the server; the browser sees more than the load balancer. Start with what’s cheap and honest enough, and know its blind spot.

5. Mind the tail: ratios, not averages

Never build a latency SLI on an average. Averages hide the pain that drives users away.

Why the mean lies

If 90% of requests take 100 ms and 10% take 10 seconds, the average looks tolerable — yet one in ten users is furious. A ratio SLI catches this: “proportion faster than 300 ms”. Better still, use two thresholds to pin both the typical case and the tail: 90% < 100 ms and 99% < 400 ms.

6. The starter recipe

When you sit down to do this for real (SRE Workbook’s method, compressed):

  1. Pick one application. Others can come later.
  2. Decide clearly who the users are — whose happiness you’re optimising.
  3. List their critical journeys — the common, important things they do.
  4. Sketch the architecture; classify components as request / pipeline / storage.
  5. Pick ≤5 SLI types for the most critical functionality. Don’t let perfect be the enemy of measured — ship one and iterate.

Try it in your own system

Take the SLI you drafted after Lesson 01 and sharpen it:

  • Write it explicitly as good events ÷ valid events — define both.
  • Confirm the component type and pick availability and/or latency.
  • Name where you’d measure it (server / load balancer / client) and its one blind spot.
  • If latency, choose two thresholds — a typical one and a tail one.

Bring it back and we’ll set the SLO target and compute the budget in Lesson 03.
