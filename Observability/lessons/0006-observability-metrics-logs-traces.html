Observability: Metrics, Logs & Traces

◷ ~11 min read • Level: Foundations • Prereq: Lesson 05 — you can alert on an SLO

Lessons 1–5 answered one question: is my service healthy? — with a single number. But when that number goes red at 3 a.m., you need a different answer: why? — and it’s usually a question nobody built a dashboard for. Observability is understanding a system’s internal state from its outputs well enough to ask brand-new questions.

1. Monitoring vs. observability

MonitoringObservability
Watches predefined signals for known failure modes. Lets you answer questions you didn’t anticipate.
“Is the SLO burning? Is CPU high?” — known-unknowns. “Why do only Android users in Brazil on checkout v3 get 500s?” — unknown-unknowns.
You built this in Lesson 05. What you need the moment the alert fires.
The definition that matters

Observability lets you understand a system from the outside — asking questions about it without shipping new code to answer them. A system is properly instrumented when you don’t need to add instrumentation mid-incident to find the cause. Monitoring tells you that something is wrong; observability lets you find out why — even for a failure you’ve never seen before.

2. The three signals

SignalWhat it isBest atWeak at
Metrics Numeric values aggregated over time (a time series). Cheap trends, dashboards, alerts, SLIs. Explaining one request; must stay low-cardinality.
Logs Timestamped records of discrete events. Rich detail & forensics of a specific event. Costly at volume; hard to aggregate; not request-linked by default.
Traces The path of one request across services, as a tree of spans. Localising which hop is slow or failing; causality. Usually sampled; needs context propagation.

None is sufficient alone. A span is a single unit of work (with a name, timing, and attributes like http.route or db.system); a trace stitches a request’s spans end-to-end. And a log — on its own a context-poor line — becomes far more powerful once it’s correlated with a span.

3. Together: the 3 a.m. flow

  1. Metric / SLO alert fires (your burn-rate alert) → that it’s wrong, and which SLI.
  2. Trace → find the slow/erroring requests and see which service is the culprit.
  3. Logs for that span → the exact error — why.

4. OpenTelemetry: instrument once, emit all three

OpenTelemetry (OTel) is how you make a system observable in a vendor-neutral way: one set of APIs/SDKs to emit traces, metrics, and logs, sharing context (the trace_id) and semantic conventions (standard names like http.response.status_code). Its Collector then exports to whatever backends you run — Prometheus/Grafana for metrics, Tempo or Jaeger for traces, Loki for logs.

Why it’s the right default

You instrument once and own your data — no lock-in to one vendor’s agent. Learn one API, and the three signals arrive already correlated. For a green operator, this is the single highest-leverage instrumentation decision you can make.

5. The staff nuance: three pillars ≠ observability

Having metrics, logs, and traces in three separate silos is not the same as being able to answer a novel question. Real observability means slicing by any dimension — “show me p99 for Android × Brazil × checkout-v3” — which needs high-cardinality, high-dimensionality context (rich span attributes, wide structured events) and correlation across signals. Collecting three data types but being unable to ask that question is just three dashboards.

The cardinality reality (→ Lesson 07)

Metrics are cheap because their labels are low-cardinality. Tag a Prometheus metric with a raw user_id and you mint a new time series per user — an explosion that can take down your metrics system. Rule of thumb: low-cardinality dimensions → metrics; high-cardinality detail → traces & logs (or wide events). Designing that split is the next lesson.
