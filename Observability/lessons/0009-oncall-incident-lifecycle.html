Reliability & SRE · Lesson 09

On-Call & the Incident Lifecycle

◷ ~12 min read • Level: Judgment • Prereq: Lesson 05 — something paged you

You can measure reliability and see why it breaks (Lessons 1–8). But when it breaks badly at 3 a.m., you need a system for responding — not heroics. Incident response is socio-technical: coordinate, mitigate, communicate, learn. For a green operator, this is the muscle to build before you’re handed a pager.

1. The anti-pattern: the unmanaged incident

Google’s SRE book opens with Mary. Her pager explodes — a datacenter stops serving. Head down, she starts fixing. A second datacenter fails, then a third; the survivors overload. VPs demand an ETA (“increase the page size!”); colleagues are “just looking”; and off in another timezone, a well-meaning Malcolm deploys an uncoordinated “fix” that kills the remaining servers outright.

Everyone was doing their job

Nobody was incompetent. Mary was too busy firefighting to coordinate or communicate; Malcolm changed production without telling anyone. Technical firefighting without command is exactly how a one-datacenter blip becomes a total outage.

2. The fix: separate command from firefighting

Google’s incident management is built on the Incident Command System (the same ICS that fire services use). The core move: split coordinating the incident from doing the technical work. Counterintuitively, clear roles give people more autonomy — nobody has to second-guess a colleague’s lane.

RoleOwnsCrucial rule
Incident Commanderthe high-level state; delegates work; single source of truth; makes decisions.Is NOT a resolver — the IC coordinates, doesn’t fix.
Ops Leadthe hands-on investigation & mitigation.The only one who changes the system (this alone prevents “Malcolm”).
Comms Leadthe public face; periodic stakeholder updates; keeps the incident doc current.Shields responders from VP interruptions.
Planning Leadlonger-term: bugs, handoffs, tracking changes to revert later.Frees Ops to stay on the fix.
Scale the structure, don’t bloat it

You don’t need four people. For a small incident, one person is IC + Ops + Comms; you split roles out as the incident grows. Start by declaring an incident and naming an IC — even if that IC is also, for now, everyone else.

3. Severity: by impact, not by panic

A severity model keyed to user/business impact makes the response consistent — it decides who’s paged, the comms cadence, and escalation, before anyone is arguing at 3 a.m.

SevImpactResponse
SEV1broad outage / data lossall-hands, IC + exec comms, ~30-min updates
SEV2significant degradation, partialon-call team + IC, ~hourly updates
SEV3minor / limited blast radiussingle team, next business day

4. The lifecycle — and the one instinct that matters

Mitigate before you diagnose

The instinct a green operator most needs: stop the bleeding first, root-cause later. Roll back, fail over, shed load, flip a feature flag — restore service, then understand why. Users don’t care why checkout was down; they care that it works now. Diagnosis is for the postmortem.

5. Communication is the job, not a distraction

  • A steady cadence. Update on a timer — even “no change, next update in 30 minutes” is gold. Silence breeds VP phone calls.
  • One source of truth. A living incident document (concurrently editable, key facts at top) plus a status page. Don’t host it on the system you’re debugging.
  • Declare early. The bar to declare an incident should be low — declaring is cheap; heroic silence is expensive.
  • Hand off cleanly. Across shifts/timezones, transfer the IC role explicitly, with state — never just drift.

6. On-call has to be sustainable

The human on the other end of your burn-rate alert (Lesson 05) needs: actionable pages (not noise), runbooks, a humane rotation, and the authority to fix what pages them. If on-call is misery, people ignore alerts and reliability rots. Alert quality isn’t a nicety — it’s how you keep your responders sharp.
