NALSD — Designing for Reliability

◷ ~13 min read • Level: Staff+ synthesis • Prereq: the whole course

This is the capstone. Everything you’ve learned — SLOs, observability, incident response, resilience, safe change, simplicity — comes together the moment you design a system. NALSD is the Staff+ discipline of designing from concrete production numbers and operational reality, not hand-wavy boxes. It’s what “being in the room” for a design review actually requires.

1. Non-abstract means: the numbers must add up

Most design discussions are boxes and arrows. NALSD — Non-Abstract Large System Design — forces concreteness, because every system eventually runs on real machines, in real datacenters, over real networks. You develop the muscle of turning a whiteboard into concrete resource estimates: how many QPS? how much data per day? how many machines? how much RAM, bandwidth, storage?

Reliability is the top feature

Google’s stance: reliability is the most critical feature of any system, and deferring it during design means fewer features at higher cost. And a caveat that frees you up: sound assumptions matter more than perfect numbers. The value is combining many imperfect-but-reasonable estimates into a better understanding of the design.

2. The iterative loop: four questions

PhaseQuestionAsks
Basic designIs it possible?Ignoring resource limits, could this satisfy the requirements at all?
Can we do better?Faster, smaller, more efficient? O(N) → O(log N)?
Scale upIs it feasible?Within real money/hardware limits — what distributed design fits?
Is it resilient?Fails gracefully? Survives a component — or whole-datacenter — failure?

You bounce between them and iterate — a design may pass the early questions and flounder later, so you modify or replace components and go again. The final design is the end of a story of twists and turns.

3. Reason from numbers (a taste)

Take Google’s AdWords example: report an accurate click-through rate for every ad. First, state the requirements as SLOs (Lesson 03):

  • 99.9% of dashboard queries complete in < 1 second.
  • 99.9% of the time, the data shown is < 5 minutes old.

Then the scale: ~500,000 search QPS and 10,000 ad-clicks/sec. Now the numbers drive the design. At ~100 bytes/entry, 500k QPS is ~50 MB/s — roughly 4 TB of logs a day. You can’t scan that per dashboard query and still hit “< 1 second,” so you must pre-aggregate CTR in a rolling window. One machine can’t take 50 MB/s plus millions of advertisers querying — so you shard. A datacenter can fail — so you replicate (N+2), and the 5-minute freshness SLO bounds how you buffer the pipeline. The SLOs and the QPS chose the architecture.

5. This is “being in the room”

NALSD bridges architecture and SRE, and that bridge is the Staff+ job. In a design review you carry both: the design that’s elegant and monitorable, that fails gracefully, recovers fast, changes safely, and is simple enough for someone to run at 3 a.m. When you can turn a whiteboard into concrete resources and walk the completeness test, you’re no longer “a better senior engineer” — you’re operating at the next level.

Capstone challenge

Pick a system you own or want to design, and run the full method:

  • State its top SLI/SLO (Lesson 03).
  • Do the back-of-envelope: QPS, data/day, machines (+ N+2).
  • Run the four questions: possible? better? feasible? resilient?
  • Run the completeness test: measured? fails/degrades? recovers? changes safely? owned & simple?
  • Note your biggest gap — and bring the design to a real review.
You’ve built the whole loop

Fifteen lessons: define reliability → measure it (SLI/SLO/budget) → govern it (policy) → alert on it (burn rate) → observe it (signals, traces) → instrument it → respond (incidents) → learn (postmortems) → prevent failure (resilience) → change safely → keep it simple → and design it whole. That’s the measurable, non-heroic core of SRE — the operational judgment your mission set out to build.
