Async & Messaging · Lesson 10

Backpressure & Load Leveling

◷ ~15 min read · interactive • Level: Core → Staff • Cashes the promise of Lesson 01

Lesson 01 sold you a benefit: “a spike becomes queue depth, not a stampede.” This lesson cashes that check. You’ll see how a buffer absorbs bursts, what backpressure really is and where it comes from, the levers to control flow — and the one hard truth that separates a resilient design from a delayed outage: buffering is not capacity.

1. Load leveling: the buffer as a shock absorber

Load leveling is the core move: put a durable queue/log between producer and consumer, and the producer’s arrival rate is decoupled from the consumer’s processing rate. A burst doesn’t hammer the downstream in real time; it piles up as depth and the consumer drains it at its own sustainable pace. You size consumers for the sustained load, and let the buffer eat the peaks.

2. Pull vs push — where backpressure comes from

Backpressure is how a slow consumer avoids being overwhelmed. Where it comes from depends on whether the consumer pulls or the broker pushes.

  • Pull (Kafka, SQS). The consumer sends fetch requests at its own pace (Confluent: a broker handles “produce requests from the producer” and “fetch requests from the consumer”). A slow consumer simply falls behind — lag grows — but is never flooded, because the durable log is the buffer. Backpressure is automatic.
  • Push (RabbitMQ). The broker pushes deliveries to the consumer. Without a limit it will flood the consumer’s buffer. The lever is prefetch (basic.qos): it caps the number of unacknowledged messages a consumer may hold, so the broker stops sending until the consumer acks. A prefetch of 0 means infinite — the classic way to melt a consumer.
# RabbitMQ (push): bound in-flight work per consumer — the backpressure knob
spring:
  rabbitmq:
    listener:
      simple:
        prefetch: 20          # at most 20 unacked messages per consumer (NOT 0 = infinite)

# Kafka (pull): the consumer already self-paces; tune the batch it pulls
    kafka:
      consumer:
        max-poll-records: 200            # records per poll
        properties: { max.poll.interval.ms: 300000 }   # process a batch within this or you're kicked

3. The hard truth: buffering ≠ capacity

A buffer absorbs bursts, not sustained overload

Load leveling smooths temporary spikes. If producers persistently outpace consumers, no amount of buffering saves you: the queue grows unbounded, lag climbs forever, and you eventually hit memory/disk limits, dropped retention (data loss), or an OOM. Rising lag with no spike means you are under-provisioned — the answer is more consumers (up to the partition count, Lesson 03), not a bigger buffer.

This makes lag / queue depth your primary health metric: Kafka consumer lag, SQS ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible, RabbitMQ queue depth. Alert on it, and autoscale on it (e.g. KEDA scales consumers by lag) rather than on CPU.

4. Bounded buffers & load shedding

An unbounded buffer hides overload until it’s catastrophic. Bounding it surfaces the problem early. When the buffer is full, the honest move is often to shed load — as the System Design Primer advises, a bounded queue can return an error (HTTP 503) so clients back off and retry with exponential backoff, instead of the system collapsing under an ever-growing backlog. Dropping or slowing work you can’t handle beats pretending you can.

Backpressure should propagate

Good backpressure travels upstream: a full downstream buffer slows or rejects the upstream producer, which slows its caller, all the way back to a user who sees a polite “try again.” The failure mode to avoid is a middle tier that swallows overload into an unbounded queue and turns a visible slowdown into an invisible, delayed detonation.
