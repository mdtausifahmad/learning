Sponsorship

Compressed from Lesson 04•Print-friendly
Mindset

A little mentorship, much more sponsorship. Advice helps; access changes careers. Mentorship says “I’ll help you get ready”. Sponsorship says “you’re ready enough, and I’ll help the organisation see it”.

Mentorship vs sponsorship

DimensionMentorshipSponsorship
Core actionAdvice and feedbackSpend capital to create opportunity
SettingPrivate conversationPublic or decision-making room
CurrencyTime, experienceCredibility, trust, reputation
OutputSkills, understandingStretch work, visibility, promotion evidence
Risk to youLowReal: your judgement is attached
Risk to themDependence if overdoneExposure without support if careless

Weekly sponsorship menu (scripts)

  • Suggest a lead — “Nadia has the right context to lead the migration workstream.”
  • Suggest a facilitator — “Mateo handled the incident well; he should facilitate the follow-up.”
  • Suggest a speaker — “This would be stronger if Chen presented the architecture story.”
  • Raise a name in the room — “Before we assign this, has Sam been considered?”
  • Create a stretch role — “Can Jordan own the rollout plan while I advise?”
  • Feed the manager — “Would it help if I shared feedback on Aisha’s design leadership?”
  • Forward and share their work — to a broader audience, author credited, with why it matters.
  • Protect ownership — redirect credit back to whoever did the work.

Make praise usable as evidence

Vague (useless in a packet)Specific (promotion evidence)
“She is great.”“She aligned three teams on the rollout plan, found the data-loss risk and re-sequenced the migration to avoid customer impact.”
“Thanks to Mei, rock star!”“Mei designed the cutover, ran the go/no-go and cut rollback time from hours to minutes.”

Formula: behaviour + scope (who or what was affected) + outcome (a number, if you have one).

Opportunity audit

  • Who gets the visible work? — and who only gets “safe” advice?
  • Echoed work — whose ideas get repeated by someone else in higher-status rooms?
  • Private trust — who is trusted privately but never named publicly?
  • Outside your network — who would benefit but isn’t in your usual circle?

Women and non-binary engineers are often over-mentored but under-sponsored. Promotions run on evidence, evidence comes from opportunities, so uneven opportunity means uneven outcomes. This is talent stewardship, not charity.

Create space: three steps

StepDoDon’t
1. IdentifyAsk “Who could grow from this?”Default to owning it yourself
2. ScaffoldReview drafts, rehearse, name risks, join the first stakeholder meetingTake over, or leave them exposed
3. Let goWhen it’s theirs, it stays theirsTake the microphone back at success

Mentorship done well

  • Goal — e.g. “improve design leadership for cross-team projects”.
  • Cadence + exit — time-bounded (e.g. six sessions); the relationship changes when the goal is met.
  • Mentee drives — they bring the agenda and artifacts, and practise between sessions.
  • More questions over time — specific, behavioural feedback tied to outcomes.

Autonomy-building questions

  • “What options have you considered?”
  • “What trade-off are you most worried about?”
  • “Who needs to be aligned before this meeting?”
  • “What would a Staff engineer do next?”
  • “What support do you want from me, and what should remain yours?”
Dependency smellGrowth smell
Waits for your approval before every movePredicts your questions and answers them on their own

Role model = passive influence

You are copied. Credit people by name · critique work, not people · say when evidence changed your mind · invite quieter voices without putting them on the spot · stay factual in incidents · make uncertainty explicit. Careful thinking out loud: “this service is a mess” can be heard as “leadership thinks we’re doomed”.

Traps
  • Mentoring endlessly and never spending political capital.
  • Sponsoring only people who resemble you or are already visible.
  • A stretch role with no support, called “empowerment”.
  • Taking back the microphone when the work succeeds.
  • Vague praise that can’t be used as promotion evidence.
  • Creating dependence through advice instead of autonomy through coaching.
