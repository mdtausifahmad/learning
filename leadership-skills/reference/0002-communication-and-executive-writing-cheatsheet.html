Executive Communication

Compressed from Lesson 02•Print-friendly
Mindset

The goal is alignment, not victory. Executives preprocess reality through their own model, so discover it and update it. Success means a busy leader can repeat your point, explain the trade-off and knows what action is needed.

The opening sentence

I am asking for a decision on X, with recommendation Y, because Z changed.

My recommendation is X; the trade-off is Y; I would revisit if Z happens.

SCQA opening (four bullets)

ElementPromptExample
SituationWhat is true and already accepted?We committed to migrate customer exports this half.
ComplicationWhat broke, changed or became risky?Two high-volume tenants cost 4× our estimate; cost scales with retries.
QuestionWhat must we decide?Continue as planned, or pause to redesign retries?
AnswerWhat should we do now?Pause new onboarding two weeks; redesign retries behind a flag; keep committed tenants.

Minto Pyramid rules

  • Answer first — then supporting arguments. No academic build-up.
  • ≤ 3 × 3 — at most three top arguments, each with at most three sub-points.
  • Order by importance — not chronology.
  • Appendix is not the path — if the reader needs logs or benchmarks to follow you, the narrative is failing.

One-page executive memo

SectionContent
TitleDecision or update in one sentence
AskDecision, advice, escalation, funding or awareness
RecommendationThe answer, first
Why nowSituation and complication
EvidenceThree strongest reasons
OptionsRecommended option plus alternatives
RisksTop risks and mitigations
Decision recordOwner, date, next checkpoint
AppendixDepth for reviewers who need it

Before sending, delete anything that proves you worked hard but doesn’t help the reader decide.

Nemawashi: pre-align checklist

  • Decision-maker — send them an early draft: “What would make this clearer or more decision-ready for you?”
  • Veto holders — include people who could quietly undermine the plan.
  • Operational carriers — the teams who will run it.
  • Partners — finance, security, legal or support, where relevant.
  • Trusted peer — someone who will tell you what’s confusing.
  • Meeting = confirm — the room should confirm the path, not discover it.

Six rules

RuleIn practice
Never fight feedbackPushback is data about their model, not an attack
Don’t evade problemsInforming executives early = absolution and options
No question without an answerBring a recommendation, even a tentative one
No academic presentationsDon’t build suspense toward the conclusion
No permanent decisionsReversible next step plus an explicit review point
Name the askDecision, advice, escalation, funding or awareness

Status, rewritten

EvasiveClear
“We are green, but there are some concerns.”“We are yellow because load tests failed at 60% of expected peak; we need one week to validate the mitigation.”

Pick the medium

MediumBest forWatch out for
Slack / TeamsFast awareness, simple asksAmbiguity, fragmented replies
EmailBroadcast alignment, recordLong threads hiding decisions
One-page memoExecutive decisionsImplementation detail overload
Design docDurable technical reasoningAssuming execs read every section
Live meetingTrade-offs, commitment, conflictDiscovering misalignment too late

Simple message → short async. Complex and needs durable reasoning → memo or doc. Complex, low trust or emotional → talk live. Pre-align either way, then write a decision record.

Traps
  • Treating executive questions as attacks rather than a model mismatch.
  • Bringing a problem without a recommended path.
  • Revealing the conclusion on slide 12.
  • Hiding bad news in a vague green status.
  • Sending dense design docs to people who need a decision summary.
  • Confusing pre-alignment with manipulation, or presenting “permanent” decisions.
