Leadership Skills · Reference 05

Stakeholders & Trust

Compressed from Lesson 05•Print-friendly
Mindset

Your authority is proxied from a sponsor, usually your manager. Alignment comes before autonomy. Trust is a bank account: make deposits before you need to withdraw, and spend it only on what matters.

Borrowed authority: four implications

ImplicationIn practice
Alignment precedes autonomyEarn freedom by showing your sponsor how you think
Sponsorship is conditionalYour judgement must visibly help their goals
Surprises are expensiveThey make your sponsor look uninformed or exposed
You represent more than yourselfIn cross-functional rooms you may be heard as “engineering”

Weekly sponsor update (< 200 words)

SectionExample
Outcomes“Search migration phase one done; p95 latency down 18%.”
Risks“Backfill confidence lower than expected; mitigation by Friday.”
Decisions needed“Need your call: delay launch or reduce scope.”
Stakeholder temperature“Product aligned, Support concerned, Security needs a pre-read.”
Next moves“Options doc Tuesday; alignment meeting Wednesday.”

Ambiguous? Send it anyway: Here is what I know, what I don’t know, what I’m doing next, and when I’ll update you.

Don’t let your sponsor surprise you: ask

  • “What are your top three priorities this quarter?”
  • “What promise have you made upward that my work most affects?”
  • “Which stakeholders are most sensitive to this area?”
  • “What risks would make you intervene?”
  • “What would make this work obviously successful in your forum?”

Managing-up rhythm

  • Weekly written update — short, regular, decision-oriented.
  • Running list — of decisions and assumptions.
  • Leadership context — ask what changed.
  • Preview escalations — before they happen, never in the meeting.
  • After key meetings — send the interpretation you’re acting on.
  • Bring options — “Three paths, the trade-off I recommend, the decision I need.”

Political capital ledger

DepositsWithdrawals
Reliability, kept commitmentsAsking people to take risks or change plans
Context and truthfulnessAsking them to accept a trade-off
Generosity: reviews, help, creditEscalations
Good judgement, shown over timeAsking them to trust you over local preference

Only withdrawals → you’re “expensive”. Only deposits → liked but ineffective.

Before a hard ask

  • Their goals — have I shown I understand them?
  • Deposit first — have I helped them before asking for help?
  • Trade-off, not personal — is the cost explicit?
  • Private first — did they get time to react before any public forum?
  • Worth it — does this truly matter?

Peer network (build it before the fire)

NetworkPurpose
Staff+ peers internallyPolitics and precedent
Product and Design leadsShape trade-offs before plans harden
Ops, Support, Security, LegalRisks engineering misses
External (e.g. Rands Leadership Slack)Reduce isolation, widen your pattern library

Cross-functional partners

PartnerNeeds from you
ProductFeasible options, sequencing, risk, user impact
DesignConstraints early enough to shape the experience
ExecutivesRecommendation, business trade-off, confidence level
Support / SuccessOperational consequences, customer messaging

Distinguish “I think…” from “Engineering is concerned…”. Only say the second after gathering engineering input. Late feedback sounds like obstruction.

Disagree while staying aligned (script)

  • 1. Shared goal — “We both want the launch to land safely.”
  • 2. Risk to that goal — “My concern is the backfill under peak load.”
  • 3. A path — “We could stage it by tenant behind a flag.”
  • 4. Missing constraints — “What am I not seeing?”
  • 5. Commit — once decided, align visibly (unless ethics or safety are at stake).

Surprise postmortem (15 min)

What signal was missed? · Who learned too late? · Which reporting path failed? · What cadence change prevents a repeat? You own the stakeholder system: who must know, who can block, who is affected, who can amplify, who needs pre-alignment.

Traps
  • Treating political capital as dirty rather than the currency of trust.
  • Confusing autonomy with independence from your sponsor.
  • Updating only when green, so red feels like a betrayal.
  • Speaking as “engineering” without gathering engineering input.
  • Escalating publicly before pre-aligning privately.
  • Spending capital on style preferences; expecting a manager safety net.
