# 05 · Promotion Playbook

How to actually *get the title* — the "make it happen" mechanics. The core truth first:

> **The promotion is a lagging indicator.** A promo packet's job is to prove you are **already operating at the next level** — "evidence of what you have done," not "a wish list of what you will do." The strongest packets show **6–12 months of sustained next-level work, not a single heroic moment.**[^1]

---

## The 9-step playbook

1. **Reframe.** A Staff engineer isn't "a better Senior engineer" — it's someone fulfilling a Staff *archetype*. Chasing more/faster Senior work won't get you there.[^2]
2. **Self-assess brutally.** Map yourself line-by-line to your company's ladder (or a public one), marking each competency "Achieving / Needs Work," backed by PR/doc links. Update every 1–2 months.[^3]
3. **Write your promo packet *early*** — "long before you think you're likely to be promoted," like a brag document. It becomes "the map to accomplishing your goal."[^4]
4. **Bring your manager in.** Take the (even empty) packet to a 1:1: *"Attaining Staff is a goal of mine — what's missing, what should I emphasize?"* Be honest about what you actually want (Ritu Vincent's early mistake: "telling my manager what I thought they wanted to hear").[^4]
5. **Close gaps via deliberately chosen projects.** Ask: "What projects in the next 6 months could develop [skill]?" and "What does success look like for someone getting promoted?"[^5]
6. **Build a support squad / activate sponsors** beyond your manager: skip-level, cross-team collaborators, engineers you've mentored, Staff engineers who can validate you.[^6]
7. **Perform at the next level consistently, and make it visible.** Committees promote people *already performing* at the next level, not those who *might*.[^5]
8. **Manager converts packet → company format and advocates in calibration.** The committee's core question: *"Do we know this person and do we trust their credibility?"*[^5]
9. **Iterate across cycles** (and manager changes). Promotions are "built over quarters, halves, and years"; re-review the packet in career 1:1s — this especially mitigates lost progress after a manager change.[^4]

---

## The promotion packet — structure

**StaffEng's minimal 4 questions:**[^4]
1. What are your **Staff projects**?
2. What are the **high-leverage ways you've impacted** the organization?
3. **Who have you mentored**, and through what accomplishments?
4. What **glue work** have you done?

**A fuller, committee-oriented template:**[^7]
- **§1 Executive summary (1 page)** — state the target level explicitly; reference 2–3 headline projects.
- **§2 Scope & impact** — prove impact *beyond your immediate team*; 2–3 projects in **STAR-E** (Situation → Task → Action → Result → **Extrapolation**/lasting org impact). Scope ladder: Senior = single-team feature; Staff = multi-team initiative, 2–4 quarter horizon, director/VP stakeholders; Principal = org/company-wide, 1–2 yr strategy, C-suite stakeholders.
- **§3 Technical leadership** — RFCs authored/shaped (with outcomes), standards you drove, specific mentorship ("mentored X → promoted to Y"), non-obvious judgment calls that proved correct.
- **§4 Cross-team influence** — multi-team initiatives led *without managerial authority*, org-wide process/tool adoption, conflicts resolved.
- **§5 Organizational contribution** — hiring, culture, knowledge-sharing (talks/blogs/docs), process improvement.
- **§6 Peer feedback** — 3–5 endorsements spanning relationship types (teammate for *depth*, cross-team peer for *breadth*, senior/skip for *judgment*, mentee for *growth-of-others*).

**Packet workflow:**[^4] draft v1 in ~1 hr → wait two days, edit → **peer-edit with Staff+ colleagues** ("peers are often better at identifying your strengths than you are") → **manager-edit asking them to enumerate the gaps** → **periodically review in career 1:1s.**

**Anti-patterns to avoid:**[^7] writing it the week before review; listing *responsibilities* instead of *impact*; claiming team wins solo ("Be precise: 'I designed X; the team implemented Y'"); no metrics; only technical work (missing leadership); ignoring the company rubric; showing "one good quarter" instead of a level shift; underselling scope.

## The "Staff project" — myth, reality, how to land one

**Reality check:** *most* engineers do **not** complete a single defining "Staff project" — a large minority do (disproportionately those promoted internally where they "grew up"). Others accumulate a track record over time, or switch companies. Some Staff engineers are "incredibly effective gurus and routers" rather than big-project runners.[^8]

**But many companies enforce it as a soft gate** — often surfaced *during* the promo meeting, "sometimes to the surprise of both the manager and the would-be Staff engineer." Maintaining your packet in advance surfaces this early.[^8]

**What makes something a genuine Staff project (3 markers):**[^8]
1. **Complex & ambiguous** — starts poorly-scoped ("the aging monolith is crippling product development").
2. **Numerous, divided stakeholders** — no alignment on problem *or* solution.
3. **A named bet where failure matters** — discussed at all-hands; success *and* failure highly visible.

*Example:* Ritu Vincent's Dropbox multi-account project spanned "kernel all the way to the user interface," estimated 6 months, took 18.[^8]

**How to get access to one:** (1) stay aligned with leadership, (2) be *known* to have the technical aptitude (needs visibility), (3) your company actually having a pressing Staff-level need (least in your control). Do one even if not required — "they stretch and grow you in a way other work won't."[^8]

## The 4 levers that actually move promotions

### Lever A — Work on what matters
Avoid **snacking** (easy + low-impact), **preening** (low-impact + high-*visibility* — "many companies conflate high-visibility and high-impact"), and **chasing ghosts** (high-effort + low-impact). Invest in **existential risks** first; otherwise areas that matter *and still have room + attention*. This is also "the single best way to create internal visibility."[^9]

### Lever B — Visibility
*"You've probably already got the technical chops; what you need to do is work on your reputation… you can't get to Staff without a good reputation."* Tactics: write/distribute **long-lived docs**; lead company forums (architecture reviews, all-hands); cheerlead peers; **share weekly notes**; contribute to the company blog; host office hours. **Executive visibility is the most important kind** — build a relationship with your manager's manager: "they rarely support folks whose work they don't know." The canonical artifact is the **brag document.**[^10]

### Lever C — Sponsorship
*"Promotions are a team activity… Don't play team games alone, you'll lose."* Your **#1 team member is you; #2 is your organizational sponsor — for Staff+ this almost always must be your direct manager.** You often need a *second* sponsor — invest in your skip-level: "if they can't remember your work's impact in a meeting two months from now, you're unlikely to get promoted." Activating a sponsor is ongoing — *"Ask your sponsor how you can support their sponsorship."*[^6]

### Lever D — Get in the room (and stay there)
To *enter*: bring something useful **the room doesn't already have**; have a **sponsor** to grant membership; make sure your sponsor **knows you want in**. Lower the cost of including you: stay aligned with your manager, optimize for the group, speak concisely, be low-friction, come prepared. Ways you get *kicked out*: misreading the room's purpose, being dogmatic, refusing to "disagree and commit," sucking the oxygen out, embarrassing your sponsor, not showing up.[^11]

## The committee / calibration process

Most performance systems combine **career ladders** + **performance designations** (rated against ladder+level, often plotted on a **nine-block** performance × trajectory grid) + **performance cycles** (1×, 2×, or 4× per year). Promotions are decided in **calibration**, so time your packet to be ready *before* it. A good ladder "allows folks to accurately self-assess."[^12]

## Title vs scope — organizational realities

- **Senior is the terminal "career level."** Systems that once helped you can turn into gatekeeping.[^2]
- **Opportunity is unevenly distributed** — infra vs product, HQ vs remote. Changing the system is possible *only with a senior sponsor*.[^2]
- **A "semi-permeable boundary":** those who *seem like* they're already part of leadership have an easier time; ~half the women Larson interviewed did "everything right" and still hit structural friction.[^2]
- **Titles mean different things across companies** (Skyscanner Principal ≈ 1 level above Senior; Uber Principal ≈ 4 levels above). Comp is "trimodal" — a title *cut* can come with a comp *raise* moving up a company tier. Research the target level; **respectfully challenge leveling** with evidence.[^13]
- **Does the title even matter?** Yes — 3 concrete benefits: (1) bypass "informal gauges of seniority" (stop re-proving yourself), (2) access to "the room," (3) higher current + lifetime comp.[^14]
- **~⅓ switched companies to get the title.** If there's no "oxygen" at the next level after genuine effort, moving is legitimate.[^2][^15]
- **The management "pendulum" detour** is optional, not required — most Staff+ never manage, but a stint can broaden you. Don't take it "for the wrong motivations."[^16]
